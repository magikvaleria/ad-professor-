structure: |
  A 60-second linear journey — uphill, downhill — directed by Ridley Scott on Gold Hill in Shaftesbury, Dorset. A baker's apprentice (Carl Barlow) pushes his bike up the cobbled hill, delivers the bread, then freewheels back down. The voiceover is the same boy, sixty years later, in a soft West Country accent. The score is the Largo from Dvořák's New World Symphony — the "Goin' Home" theme — performed by the Ashington Colliery Band, which adds its own working-class English warmth to the music. The product appears for less than three seconds, at the kitchen threshold, after the feeling is finished forming.
template: |
  1. Pick a chore or journey humble enough to feel timeless — a delivery, a meal, an early-morning walk. Not a product use-case. A life-use-case.
  2. Cast a young protagonist. Voice it with an elder remembering. The viewer auto-translates the elder's voice into their own future-self.
  3. Use a piece of music older than the intended viewer, and not from their region. Unfamiliar timecode lends instant antiquity. The viewer can't place the era exactly, so the era becomes "always."
  4. Build sensory specificity at the level of texture (cobblestone, woodsmoke, the angle of late light, the steam off a loaf) — but withhold brand-world detail. The world must feel found, not built.
  5. Let the journey end at a domestic threshold: a kitchen, a porch, a doorstep, a table.
  6. Reveal the product only at the threshold. The viewer arrives feeling they have *returned*, not been sold to. The brand becomes the keeper of the memory the ad just installed.
appliedExample: |
  Two examples — one in a totally different category (to test that the template generalizes), one in the actual brand it's about to be used for (to test that it fits).

  **A — Different category: a small DTC bedding company.**

  A young woman, mid-twenties, in a sunlit second-floor room, hanging a fresh linen sheet to dry by a window. The fabric moves slowly in the breeze. No music for the first six seconds, then a piano line that could be 1962 or 1978 but isn't either. Voiceover, an older woman, calm Midwestern: "I never noticed at the time. But the years I slept well were the years everything else got easier. I bought one set of sheets in 1979 and I bought one set in 2008. I'd have spent the money sooner if I'd known what the money was for." Cut to the woman's hand smoothing the made bed. A single woven label appears for a second on the seam — no logo, no URL.

  **B — Same category, real brand: Nature's Shimmer Watercolor Workshop, $47.**

  A woman in her early forties at her own kitchen table. Late afternoon. She is mixing a saucer of water with a thumb of gold pigment. The light catches the pigment as she tilts the saucer. No music for the first eight seconds — just the small sound of brush against ceramic.

  Voiceover, an older woman, perhaps her own future self, in a soft warm English accent: "I'd forgotten how light moves through pigment. I'd forgotten my own hands. There was a Sunday afternoon I almost lit a candle and got out the box, and didn't — and I would have lit the candle thirty years sooner if I'd known what it was *for*."

  Final shot: the painted square of cotton paper drying on the kitchen table, beside a handwritten card. The card reads "Nature's Shimmer · four Sundays · $47." No logo. No CTA. The music — a Dvořák-adjacent slow strings line — comes in only on the last beat.

  Same template both times. The elder voice describing the protagonist's present moment is the engine. The product is the threshold the journey ends at, not the destination. Notice how much heavier the withholding feels here than in a normal DTC ad: the workshop is a low-stakes purchase that lives or dies on whether the buyer feels she's been *waiting* for it. The Hovis template gives her permission to feel that she has been.

Why this template still works almost fifty-three years later: the ad sells nothing functional about bread. It sells a moment the viewer has never had and now will not be able to stop having. Every Hovis loaf bought between 1973 and the present has been partly a renewal of that single afternoon on Gold Hill.

The genius is the voice. Most ads that aim for nostalgia cast a narrator who sounds like the protagonist — a peer voice, a contemporary register — and the result is sentimentality. Hovis casts a narrator who is the protagonist *sixty years later*, and the result is *memory*. The grammar of an old man recalling his own childhood is the grammar of inheritance, not advertising. The viewer slips inside the inheritance without noticing.

What to steal: the structural move of using an elder voice to describe a younger protagonist's present moment, ending the journey at a domestic threshold, withholding the product until the feeling is fully formed. What to leave behind: the period setting. The template works for a 2026 kitchen table just as well as for a 1932 cobblestone hill — what matters is the *grammar of remembering*, not the era being remembered.

Note on the cosmology: Apple's Think Different met the Saturn-in-Aries current (lone individual proving they're real). Hovis meets the Jupiter-in-Cancer current (the longing to be received). Two ads, two ends of the same cosmological axis, both season-correct. The Ad Professor archive is sturdier with both poles represented.
